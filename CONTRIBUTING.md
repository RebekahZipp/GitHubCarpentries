# Reviewing and Contributing to GitHub Carpentries

This lesson is being built with the tools and community practices it teaches.

Carpentries instructors are invited first as a **community of practice**: examine the lesson, test its reasoning, identify assumptions, contribute observations, and help assess its rigor. The immediate goal is insight and peer review, not rewriting someone else's lesson.

## Start here

Read the root `README.md`, then:

- `PEDAGOGY_RUBRIC.md` — reasoning, prompts, intentional mistakes, feedback, repetition, and handoff rubric
- `student/README.md` — learner-facing path
- `instructor/README.md` — teaching path
- `instructor/LESSON_LOG.md` — development provenance and teaching cases

## What are we reviewing?

Please examine the work for:

- pedagogical rigor and alignment with Carpentries practice;
- technical accuracy;
- novice accessibility and low-level language;
- sequencing and pacing;
- missing assumptions or conceptual jumps;
- whether the **why, what, and how** are explicit;
- whether prompts invite reasoning without requiring a response;
- whether mistakes expose a concept and include a recovery and learner handoff;
- whether repetition deepens reasoning rather than only repeating commands;
- whether feedback is built into the technical lesson;
- whether helpers are used as knowledge conduits;
- troubleshooting and recovery;
- clear distinctions among Git, GitHub, RStudio, and the Terminal;
- reproducibility and provenance of teaching decisions;
- accessibility and inclusive teaching; and
- whether learners leave with an authentic repository they can continue developing.

A central question remains:

**Can another instructor understand, evaluate, and teach from these materials without reconstructing the reasoning that produced them?**

A second question is now equally important:

**Does the lesson gradually transfer the reasoning from instructor to learner?**

## Review before revision

At this stage, please use Issues for observations, questions, critique, and recommendations.

Useful review comments explain:

1. what you noticed;
2. where it occurs;
3. why it matters for a learner, helper, or instructor;
4. what evidence supports the concern; and
5. what you would consider or test.

A Pull Request can come later when a specific contribution has been discussed or invited. The author remains responsible for deciding what becomes part of the lesson.

## Four connected rhythms

### Learner workflow

**CHANGE → INSPECT → CHOOSE → RECORD → REVIEW → SHARE**

### Reasoning and recovery

**EXPECT → OBSERVE → EXPLAIN → TEST → ACT → VERIFY**

### Instructor observation

**ACTION → RESULT → CONCEPT → TEACHING MOMENT → RECOVERY → HANDOFF**

### Community of practice

**PRACTICE → DOCUMENT → SHARE → QUESTION → REFLECT → REFINE → REUSE**

The student learns the workflow.

The instructor makes the reasoning visible.

Helpers surface knowledge from around the room.

The community examines and strengthens the practice.

## Prompts and participation

Questions are invitations, not quizzes. A small or quiet class should still work: the instructor can pose the prompt, pause briefly, and continue by modeling the answer aloud.

When learners or helpers contribute, their reasoning should be taken seriously and checked against the evidence when practical.

Reviewers should therefore look for both problems:

- a script that never leaves room for the room; and
- a script that depends on learners answering every question before it can continue.

## Why keep mistakes and reasoning?

The reasoning is part of the work.

A command alone records what was typed. The lesson log records why an action mattered, what happened, what concept it exposed, how the instructor recovered, and what learners should be able to do afterward.

Mistakes are useful when they are safe, realistic, conceptually purposeful, and followed by evidence-based recovery. They should not be theatrical traps for learners.

Peer review adds another layer: what another instructor notices that the original instructor may not see.

Git records changes. GitHub supports conversation around changes. A community of practice gives those conversations meaning.
